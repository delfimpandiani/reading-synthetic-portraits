# ctrl.alt.img Synthetic Situation Worksheet

This document is a completed example of the general **Synthetic Situation Worksheet**. It records the technical, textual, social, institutional, and ethical conditions through which the ctrl.alt.img portrait corpus was produced. It provides contextual detail that the accompanying chapter can reference without reproducing in full.

## Project and corpus

| Field | Information |
|---|---|

# 1. System and technical conditions

## What produced the images?

The images were produced through **ctrl.alt.img**, an interactive AI photo-booth installation developed by affect lab. Each visit generated one synthetic portrait through a sequence involving facial classification, editable demographic fields, participant free-text self-description, a fixed prompt template, and an image-generation system integrated into the booth.

## Which model, platform, interface, or installation is involved?

The installation consisted of a black, enclosed photo booth with an interactive screen. It used a legacy Stable Diffusion-derived image-generation system integrated into the booth workflow. Visitors interacted with the screen, completed the system’s classification and self-description steps, and received a generated portrait on the same screen.

## Which version, checkpoint, pipeline, or settings are known?

The available information identifies the system as a Stable Diffusion-derived generator associated with an earlier phase of public diffusion-model development. The images show recurring visual characteristics associated with that period, including unstable bodily detail, distortions, and uncanny or incomplete rendering of faces, hands, bodies, objects, and spatial relations.

The prompt structure appears to have remained stable throughout the collection period. To the best of the researchers’ knowledge, the model version also remained stable while the booth travelled between venues.

## Which technical details remain unknown?

The exact model checkpoint, pipeline, generation settings, sampler, seed handling, guidance values, safety filters, negative prompts, fine-tuning, system prompt, and embedded stylistic instructions cannot now be recovered. These unknowns limit the exact reproducibility of the generation process and restrict claims about the technical origin of particular visual patterns.

## Which filters, post-processing steps, or safety settings are known?

The research team received raw portraits generated by the booth, accompanied by their associated prompts. The portraits were displayed to visitors immediately after generation and were supplied to the researchers without researcher-led post-processing. Information about model-side content filtering, internal prompt processing, or image-generation settings is unavailable.

## Technical conditions: analytic implications

The system should be understood as a legacy diffusion-based image generator activated through a particular public installation. The corpus provides a time-specific record of this arrangement. It does not support direct claims about current image-generation systems, nor does it provide a baseline of unprompted or unscaffolded model behaviour.

# 2. Prompting and classification

## Which prompt template or default instructions shape outputs?

The installation assembled visitor inputs into a fixed English-language prompt template approximately structured as follows:

> “A photo of a [ethnic descriptor] [gender] who is [age] years old who is also [free-text self-description] sitting in a photo booth with a black background.”

The closing clause, “sitting in a photo booth with a black background,” provides explicit visual guidance. It likely contributed to recurring chairs, seated bodies, portrait framing, interior scenes, black backgrounds, and related photo-booth conventions.

An example prompt from the corpus is:

> “A photo of a white man who is 29 years old who is also a italian, handsome, bearded, big nosed, normcore sitting in a photo booth with a black background.”

## Which user-provided text or image inputs are involved?

Visitors entered between one and five free-text descriptors of their choice. The system did not provide a controlled vocabulary, and visitors selected their own separators, commonly commas. Free-text entries could concern appearance, personality, cultural or national affiliation, clothing, objects, activities, gestures, poses, or further image instructions.

The free-text field therefore added visitor-authored language to the fixed prompt structure. Some entries could influence visual styling or content directly, including through instructions about clothes, accessories, props, actions, or pose.

## Which demographic categories, classifiers, labels, or defaults structure the system?

The first stage of the booth interaction was a facial scan. The system used the scan to estimate ethnicity, binary gender, and age.

Ethnicity was assigned through a highest-percentage match from five predefined categories:

- White;
- Black;
- Asian;
- Latino Hispanic;
- Middle Eastern.

Gender was predicted through a binary classification:

- male;
- female.

Age was predicted as a numerical value.

These three predicted fields entered the prompt structure. The facial scan supplied the demographic prompt fields and did not otherwise directly enter image generation.

## Which inputs can users revise?

Visitors could view and manually edit the predicted ethnicity, gender, and age fields before continuing to the free-text description stage. The available records do not establish whether edits occurred or how often visitors changed any of these fields.

## Which categories may carry political, historical, or ethical consequences?

The five ethnicity categories and binary gender classification structured the terms through which visitors became computationally legible. These categories have racialising and cisheteronormative effects. They did not function as neutral metadata: the classifier produced an initial account of the visitor, and that account entered the prompt used to generate the portrait.

The possibility of editing these categories created some space for visitor intervention. The classificatory frame still shaped the interaction, the available terms of recognition, and the image-production process.

## Prompting and classification: analytic implications

Each portrait emerged through an interaction among classifier outputs, participant edits where made, participant free-text input, the fixed template, and the image-generation system. Features observed in the corpus cannot be attributed to the model alone. They may relate to the demographic fields, free-text descriptors, the photo-booth and black-background instruction, interface conditions, model behaviour, and their interaction.

# 3. Participants and setting

## When and where were the images produced?

The first image in the corpus was generated on **24 May 2024**. The final image in the corpus was generated on **10 February 2025**.

During this period, ctrl.alt.img travelled through museums, exhibitions, and a festival setting in the Netherlands.

| Venue | Location | Installation dates |
|---|---|---|
| Foam Photography Museum | Amsterdam | 1 June–8 September 2024 |
| Into The Great Wide Open | Vlieland | 29 August–1 September 2024 |
| Forum | Groningen | 13–22 September 2024 |
| Noorderlicht | Groningen | 26 October 2024–19 January 2025 |
| Nederlands Fotomuseum | Rotterdam | 28 September 2024–25 May 2025 |

The venue dates overlap. An individual image’s generation date therefore does not always establish the precise location where it was produced.

## Who participated in producing, prompting, selecting, curating, or collecting the images?

Participation was open to visitors in the environments where the booth was installed. The visitor supplied a face for classification, had the option to edit demographic predictions, entered free-text descriptors, viewed the resulting portrait, and could choose to email it to themselves.

The installation developer designed the booth, video, interface, classifier workflow, prompt structure, and generation process. The research team received the resulting images and prompts after collection and did not participate in the installation’s original data collection or design.

To the researchers’ knowledge, visitors generally interacted with the booth without a human facilitator inside it. Some prompt content suggests that a small number of visitors may have entered the booth together.

## Which institutions, platforms, venues, or communities shaped participation?

The installation moved between cultural venues with different publics and institutional contexts. Foam and Noorderlicht hosted the booth within museum or exhibition environments, while Into The Great Wide Open placed it within a festival site. These settings likely shaped who encountered the installation, how much time they spent with it, how they understood its purpose, and the kinds of descriptors they entered.

## Which language, accessibility, or technical requirements shaped participation?

The booth used English-language instructions and an English-language prompt structure. Participation required visitors to hear or understand the video and screen instructions, engage with the facial-scan and demographic-classification sequence, type free-text descriptors into the interface, and navigate its available categories.

These requirements may have shaped who participated and how participants described themselves. The corpus should therefore not be treated as a representative sample of a broad population.

## What happened during a booth visit?

A visit followed this sequence:

1. The visitor entered the enclosed black booth and encountered an interactive screen.
2. An informational video introduced the project and its context.
3. A later part of the video presented labels entered by previous visitors as possible inspiration.
4. A facial scan estimated ethnicity, gender, and age.
5. The system displayed these predictions and allowed edits.
6. The visitor entered between one and five free-text descriptors.
7. The installation assembled a prompt from the demographic fields, free-text descriptors, and fixed photo-booth clause.
8. The image-generation system rendered a portrait.
9. The portrait appeared immediately on the screen.
10. The visitor could email the generated image to themselves.
11. The visitor was invited to select one of five predefined emotional responses to the portrait.

The emotional-response data were unavailable to the researchers.

## What do available prompt fields suggest about the coded subset?

Within the random 200-image coded subset, the structured descriptors most frequently indicated White women (n = 97) and White men (n = 74). Other entries included Asian descriptors (n = 17), Latino Hispanic descriptors (n = 10), Black descriptors (n = 9), and Middle Eastern descriptors (n = 7).

Some prompts included more than one demographic descriptor. These counts therefore exceed the number of images and should not be read as a demographic distribution of verified participants.

The average prompted age was 31.83 years. The prompted age range extended from 11 to 75. These values reflect the system’s age estimate and possible participant edits. They do not establish verified age.

## Participants and setting: analytic implications

The corpus captures public interaction with a particular installation. Venue, participant population, English-language interface, classifier categories, video instructions, self-description practice, and photo-booth framing all form part of the conditions through which the portraits were produced.

# 4. Data and ethics

## What is the provenance of the corpus?

The research team received a corpus of portraits generated by ctrl.alt.img, accompanied by associated prompts. The team did not design the installation or gather the data during the original public interaction.

## Which data can be shared, and which require restrictions?

The full portrait corpus and raw prompt data should remain restricted by default. The repository can share the general protocol, synthetic-situation documentation, taxonomy diagram, public-facing codebook, aggregate results, and derived outputs that have been reviewed for disclosure risk.

## Could prompts, images, or metadata create identification or exposure risks?

Yes. Free-text prompts can include self-description, identity claims, cultural or national affiliation, distinctive combinations of characteristics, and other potentially identifying or sensitive material. Portrait images may also create unwanted exposure if they are recirculated outside the installation’s original context.

Some generated portraits may contain sexualised, racialised, distorted, or otherwise harmful visual representations. Public circulation can reproduce or intensify these harms.

## Which permissions, agreements, or governance arrangements apply?

Any sharing of images, prompts, image identifiers, or metadata should follow the data-governance arrangements under which the corpus was provided, alongside applicable institutional procedures and ethical review. This worksheet does not establish permission for unrestricted public release.

## Data and ethics: analytic implications

Research transparency requires documentation of the method and its evidence. It does not require unrestricted disclosure of participant-generated material. Aggregate outputs, public codebook documentation, methodology, and ethics notes can support scrutiny while reducing avoidable exposure.

# 5. Analytic implications

## Which conditions may shape style, composition, identity categories, or output patterns?

Several components of the ctrl.alt.img arrangement can shape visual patterns:

- the five-category ethnicity classifier;
- binary gender prediction;
- numerical age prediction;
- visitor edits to these fields, where made;
- participant free-text self-description;
- possible free-text instructions about clothing, props, action, or pose;
- the fixed prompt template;
- photo-booth framing;
- the explicit black-background clause;
- the booth interface and instructional video;
- the cultural setting and visitor population at each venue;
- the legacy diffusion-based image-generation system;
- unknown model settings and possible embedded stylistic instructions.

## Which visual patterns relate directly to the template?

The clause “sitting in a photo booth with a black background” likely contributed to chairs, seated bodies, interior scenes, portrait framing, black backgrounds, and related photo-booth aesthetics. These features should be interpreted as part of the installation’s explicit prompt arrangement.

## Which patterns remain analytically relevant beyond the fixed photo-booth clause?

Beautification, revealed skin, body-size emphasis, averted gaze, hand-to-face pose, hand signs, crotch-shot framing, and bodily distortions were not explicitly requested by the fixed photo-booth and black-background clause. Their occurrence remains analytically relevant, while their origins may involve interactions among participant input, structured prompt fields, template design, model behaviour, and unknown technical settings.

## What is the object of analysis?

The object of analysis is the **ctrl.alt.img synthetic situation**: a model activated through a particular arrangement of classification, self-description, prompting, interface, exhibition setting, participation, and image generation.

There is no unscaffolded model output in this corpus. The classifier, prompt template, participant text, interface, photo-booth framing, venue, and model jointly shape the resulting portraits.

## Which claims should be treated cautiously?

The corpus does not support direct causal claims about:

- individual demographic descriptor fields;
- participant free-text input;
- the prompt template;
- model architecture or training data;
- model-side filtering;
- venue effects;
- contemporary text-to-image systems;
- the verified demographic identity or self-understanding of participants.

Descriptive patterns can guide close analysis and theoretical interpretation. Their meaning remains tied to the conditions documented in this worksheet.

## Summary statement

ctrl.alt.img provides a situated case for studying representational power in synthetic portraiture. Each portrait was produced through a stacked sociotechnical arrangement of facial classification, constrained demographic categories, editable fields, participant self-description, a fixed photo-booth prompt, a black-background instruction, public cultural venues, and a legacy diffusion-based image generator. The resulting corpus makes it possible to develop and test a theory-informed feature codebook, while requiring careful limits on claims about prompts, models, participants, and representational harm.
